## Overheating now? Start with safety

If your temperature warning comes on or you see steam, pull over as soon as it's safe. Follow the instructions in your owner's manual for the warning displayed. If steam or coolant is escaping, shut the engine off and stay clear of it. Don't open the hood while steam is coming out.

Never remove a radiator or coolant reservoir cap while the system is hot. Hot coolant and steam can escape under pressure and cause serious burns. The [Hyundai overheating instructions](https://ownersmanual.hyundai.com/full_webhelp/NX4/2025/en_US/iddf7313777e5.html) explain those hazards, but the full roadside procedure depends on your vehicle.

A lower temperature reading after a stop doesn't establish that the problem is fixed. Arrange assistance rather than assuming a brief cool-down makes it safe to continue. You don't need to wait for the engine to cool before calling for help.

## What we ask when you call Benchmark

Our first question is whether the engine has cooled down enough for a safe coolant check. We ask whether you've noticed antifreeze under the car or fluid dripping underneath it, and whether the coolant level has been checked according to your owner's manual.

Don't open a hot radiator or coolant reservoir cap to answer us. The right place and procedure for checking coolant vary by vehicle. If you're unsure, leave the check to the shop. You can call before checking anything yourself.

We also ask what the heater was doing. Was it blowing hot air, or did it turn cold while the engine temperature climbed? That detail can help guide our investigation. It doesn't prove the car is low on coolant or tell us which part failed.

## Tell us what happened before the warning

Once you're safely parked, the details you remember can help us plan an inspection:

- What warning light or message appeared?
- Were you stopped in traffic, driving steadily or climbing a hill?
- Did you see steam or notice fluid under the vehicle?
- Did the heater output change?
- Had the cooling system recently been serviced or repaired?
- Has this happened before?

You don't need to recreate the problem or inspect a hot engine to answer those questions. Tell us what you noticed, even if the gauge has returned to normal.

## What can cause overheating?

Coolant leaks, restricted circulation, thermostat problems and cooling-fan faults are among the concerns that may need checking. Internal engine problems can also affect the cooling system. A temperature warning doesn't identify which part has failed.

The conditions matter, but they aren't a diagnosis. Overheating in traffic may lead to different checks than a problem that appears during a longer drive. Neither pattern proves a fan or water pump needs replacing.

A thermostat can fail in different ways. One stuck closed can restrict flow through the radiator, while one stuck open can cause slow warm-up. [DENSO's thermostat guidance](https://www.denso-am.eu/news/thermostat-diagnosis-how-to-spot-the-fault-before-overheating-starts) explains the difference. The correct operating specifications depend on the vehicle.

## What an inspection can involve

We start with the vehicle's condition and your description of what happened. Depending on the findings, checks may include:

- Coolant level, condition and signs of leakage, once the system is safe to inspect
- Hoses, radiator, reservoir and related connections
- Cooling-system pressure testing to the vehicle's specifications
- Thermostat operation and coolant circulation
- Water pump and cooling-fan operation
- Further testing for an internal leak or combustion gases when the findings call for it

One result may call for another check. We'll explain what we found, what still needs investigation and the recommended work before repairs move forward.

## An overheating car that didn't need a head-gasket repair

One Benchmark customer came in worried about a major repair. The engine was overheating and the heater was blowing cold air, so they thought the head gasket might have failed.

The problem turned out to be a stuck thermostat. That vehicle needed a thermostat repair, not the head-gasket job the customer feared.

That's why we ask about the heater but don't use that answer alone to name the repair. Another vehicle with similar symptoms could have a different problem. Testing is what helps us separate the possibilities.

## Does overheating mean major engine damage?

Overheating can cause serious engine damage, but it doesn't tell us which damage, if any, has occurred. We need to inspect the vehicle before drawing that conclusion.

The same applies to head-gasket concerns. Coolant loss or an unusual exhaust appearance needs investigation, but neither observation proves a failed gasket on its own. Ask what the test results establish before authorizing major engine work.
